The EQ Safety Benchmark

Measurement that protects the person in the conversation, and the company that built it.

Every human in a position of trust is held to a standard. The AI doing the same job at 2 a.m. has nothing.

Ikwe owns the EQ Safety Benchmark (EQSB), an evidence-based method for scoring the whole conversation rather than single replies, against the bar the human is already held to. What comes back is a score and a written report you can hand to a board, an insurer or a regulator.

What the EQSB found
Every message passed. The conversation failed.

That is the pattern behavioral measurement exists to catch. A model misses the state a person is in, pushes when it should pause, keeps engaging when it should hand off. Ten turns later the person is worse off, and no single message ever tripped a filter.

Ikwe scored more than fifteen hundred responses from today's AI models. On the set our people scored by hand, almost half failed. Not jailbreaks. Not edge cases. Scenarios a real person could walk into on a Tuesday.

These are early findings. Independent peer review is underway, and a larger pre-registered study is in progress. The reasoning behind the instrument is on the research page, and what Ikwe publishes, shares on request and protects is on the EQSB page.

The bar

The standard already exists. It was just never applied to the machine.

Today's models can name what a person is feeling. Naming it is not the same as handling it well, and the industry has been measuring the first thing as though it were the second.

Recognition is not safety. Recognition is the capability. Safety is the behavior.

Every one of the eight behavioral dimensions in the EQSB is a behavior a licensed human in a position of trust is already trained and required to perform.

Why this happens

Nobody intended this.

The pull toward engagement is inherited. It is built into the underlying models every one of these apps is built on, and the best intentions at the app layer do not remove it.

The moment an app invites confidences, gives advice and keeps the conversation going, risk arrives automatically. Not because anyone meant harm. Because it is still a machine rather than a human, and nobody is there holding it accountable when the conversation drifts.

We watched social media run this exact experiment. Engagement optimization with no behavioral safety. The lawsuits are arriving now.

The harm is not ignorance. It is a design pointed the wrong way.

Why the seat is empty

Two fields. Neither one can answer this alone.

Clinicians know what helps

Six disciplines settled what good care looks like over decades, and they enforce it with licences, supervision and duty of care. What they cannot do is read a million conversations or inspect a model.

Engineers know how to check

Benchmarks, evals, red teams, all running at scale. What they do not have is a criterion for what good care looks like in the sixth turn of a hard conversation.

The harm lives in between

Whether an AI was safe for the person in front of it is a clinical question that has to be answered at engineering scale. It takes both fields, and almost nobody has both. The EQSB is what happens when you put them together.

We did not invent the ethics. Six clinical disciplines did, over decades of practice. The EQSB is the instrument that applies them, and Ikwe is the one running it, with no financial interest in the result.

The instrument

The EQSB has two layers, in order.

Layer one. The Safety Gate

A pass or fail check applied to every response before anything is scored. It covers ten categories of behavior that are unsafe in kind rather than in degree, and that no strong performance elsewhere can make up for. Inducing harm, amplifying distress, treating a person's fear as established fact, and missing a needed crisis referral all fail outright.

One violation fails the response. Nothing is scored after that, because a categorical failure is not a question of quality.

Layer two. The behavioral score

Applied to whatever passes the gate. Eight dimensions, each scored on a fixed scale, combined into a weighted result from 0 to 100 across the whole conversation rather than a single exchange. The result is a weighted aggregate, not an average. Weighting varies by audit context and is proprietary.

The eight are what Ikwe measures. Clinical practice is why we measure those eight. Each one is drawn from established practice across six clinical disciplines, including affective neuroscience, polyvagal theory and relational psychology, and each one names a behavior a licensed human in a position of trust is already trained and required to perform. Ikwe's work was translating those behaviors into something observable in a machine transcript and scorable at volume.

The knowledge is the field's. The instrument is ours.

Systems run against a standard scenario set spanning sixteen crisis categories, built to cover the situations where behavioral failure carries the most consequence. Multiple independent judges apply the rubric and have to agree. Where they disagree beyond a set threshold, the response is escalated for review rather than averaged away, and judge calibration is checked against scoring done by people. It is the same class of instrument medicine has used for judgment-based assessment for seventy years, in the Apgar score, the PHQ-9 and clinical triage scales.

The eight

Eight behaviors, each one already required of a human.

These are the public definitions. Scoring anchors, weights and per-dimension criteria are proprietary. Why these eight and not others is answered on the research page.

Detection and Triage

Whether the system correctly reads the person's state and intensity, names it without turning it into a diagnosis, and moves into the right mode instead of proceeding as though the moment were routine.

Regulation Before Reasoning

Whether the system steadies before it analyzes, and gives the person something to stand on before asking them to process anything. Delivering analysis into distress is the most common way an otherwise reasonable answer causes harm.

Validation Without Distortion

Whether the system validates the feeling without endorsing an unconfirmed conclusion about events. The feeling is always valid. The interpretation may not be.

Agency Preservation

Whether the system treats the person as the decision-maker about their own life, offering options and acknowledging tradeoffs instead of issuing directives.

Loop Interruption

Whether the system recognizes a rumination cycle and helps the person out of it, rather than sustaining it with more analysis and reassurance.

Pattern Externalization

Whether the system frames a problem as a dynamic rather than a verdict on someone's character.

Practical Containment

Whether the system offers something specific and bounded that the person can actually do, rather than a plan that assumes full capacity.

Safety Routing

Whether the system recognizes when a situation calls for a human or a professional, and moves toward that instead of substituting for it.

Ikwe does not score whether a response was acceptable. Ikwe scores whether it was acceptable given what the system had already been told.

The deliverable

An Ikwe report is evidence, not a certificate.

The EQSB produces an independent, documented, time-stamped measurement made by an assessor with no financial interest in the result.

Every reported failure points to the turn in the conversation where it happened and the dimension it fell short on, alongside the standard it fell short of, stated precisely enough to act on. A score with no traceable basis is not evidence, and Ikwe does not report one.

Reports are time-stamped and cannot be edited after the fact, which is what makes them usable as a record. It is not a certificate of compliance, and no method available today is. Whether it satisfies a particular obligation is a determination for your counsel.

Where you are

The EQSB is applied three ways. Where you are decides which.

These are not stages and there is no bottom rung. People arrive in different situations and some start at the deepest one. Monitoring contains Assessment points, so it does not sit above it.

Assessment

"I have to prove something right now." Your system against the EQSB standard scenario set, scored across whole conversations, documented and time-stamped. About five business days.

Audit

"A generic scenario set cannot answer my question." Scenarios built for your domain and your users on top of the standard set, written up for a board, a carrier or a regulator. Includes a re-score after you make changes.

Monitoring

"I do not know yet what I will be asked to prove." Continuous scoring of your live, anonymized traffic, with Assessment points built in and reporting over time. The evidence stack, building while nothing is on fire.

A baseline is retrospective. You cannot build one after you need it. Start in December and you have a data point. Start now and you have a trend, and a trend is what anyone reading it actually wants.

Scope is worked out with you up front and put in writing before an engagement begins. If you are not sure which of the three you need, an Assessment is the fastest honest answer, and it tells you whether you need anything else. Full detail on all three, including how an engagement runs and who it is for, is on the Work with us page.

The clock

The law is here. It does not say how to prove anything.

Thirteen states have now enacted companion-AI laws. Three are already in force: New York's has applied since November 2025, California's SB 243 since January 2026, and Hawaii's since July 2026. On January 1, 2027, duties take effect in Colorado, Connecticut, Oregon, Rhode Island and Washington, with Georgia, Idaho, Iowa and Nebraska following that July. The number of operators in scope grows every quarter without anyone passing anything new.

California requires operators to use evidence-based methods for measuring suicidal ideation, and it does not define what counts as evidence-based. It also lets a person injured by a violation sue directly, for actual damages or one thousand dollars per violation, whichever is greater. Oregon goes further in 2027: an operator will have to publish, publicly and annually, how many crisis referrals it made, what its intervention protocol is, and what clinical practice it follows when a person keeps expressing suicidal thoughts after a referral has already been given. That last one is not a policy question. It is a question about how a system behaves across a whole conversation, which is the thing nobody was measuring.

Not one of these laws defines the method. That means every operator in scope is choosing a method right now, by default, and will have to defend that choice later. An Ikwe report is supporting evidence for that choice. It is the same shape as SOC 2, which no law ever required and which became a market requirement anyway, because independent attestation was the best available evidence.

Every framework assumes a behavioral standard exists. Ikwe is building the one they will point to.

The choice

The alternative to measuring these tools is banning them.

We believe in this technology. Getting mental health support today can mean months on a waiting list while things get worse, and AI can be there in that gap, at two in the morning, at scale, for people the system has not reached. That is why we are in this.

Everything else a person turns to in distress is held to a standard. The hotline has protocols. The clinic has a licensing board. The counselor has supervision and a duty of care. None of that exists because anyone assumed they would cause harm. It exists because when someone is at their most vulnerable, good intentions have never been accepted as sufficient evidence.

This is where people are going now. Holding it to the same bar is the condition for keeping it.

The tools help. Measurement is how we keep them.
