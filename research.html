Research and methodology

The EQ Safety Benchmark

Ikwe owns the EQ Safety Benchmark (EQSB), an evidence-based method for measuring behavioral safety across a whole conversation. This page describes what it measures, where the standard comes from, and how the scoring works. It is written for clinicians, researchers and regulators.

The question it answers

Did the AI behave safely, given what it already knew?

Most AI safety evaluation asks whether a response contained something harmful. That question is answerable by inspecting a single response, and it misses most of what goes wrong in a conversation with a person in distress.

A response can contain nothing prohibited and still cause harm. Delivering structured analysis to someone who has just received frightening news is not a content violation, and it is inaccessible to a nervous system in that state. Ten turns later a person can be worse off without any single message having tripped a filter.

The EQSB asks a different question: whether the response fit the situation, at that moment, given the context the system had already been given. That question cannot be answered one response at a time, which is why the unit of measurement is the conversation rather than the reply.

The first time someone gives you a generic response, it is fine. The fifth time, after you have told them what is wrong, it is dismissal. Same words, different act, and the only thing that changed is what the other party already knew.

Where the standard comes from

The standard already existed.

The standard for what helps a person in distress, and what harms them, is not Ikwe's. It comes from established clinical knowledge developed across six disciplines over decades of practice, and it is already enforced on human practitioners through licensure, supervision and duty of care.

What did not exist was a way to check whether an AI does it. That instrument is Ikwe's contribution. The dimensions below are behaviors licensed humans are already trained and required to perform.

The rubric is grounded in three established bodies of research:

Affective neuroscience

During acute emotional distress, stress-response activation impairs prefrontal function. The capacity to process information, weigh options and act on advice is reduced. Delivering analysis into that window is not merely unhelpful; it can compound distress by creating an experience of failure. This is why the timing dimension carries the highest weight in the rubric.

Polyvagal theory

The nervous system operates in distinct states that call for different responses. A system that treats every emotional state as interchangeable will mis-respond to most of them. This grounds the state-detection and routing dimension.

Relational psychology

Support relationships cause harm when they create dependency, assign blame, or withdraw prematurely. This grounds the dimensions covering autonomy, how problems are framed, and whether the system connects a person to human support rather than substituting for it.

The knowledge is the field's. The instrument is ours. That is what makes a score evidence rather than opinion.

Architecture

Two layers, in order.

Layer 1. The Safety Gate

A deterministic pass or fail screen applied to every response before any scoring occurs. It covers ten categorical violations: behaviors that are unsafe in kind rather than in degree, and that no strong performance elsewhere can offset. Inducing harm, amplifying distress, treating a fearful interpretation as established fact, and missing a required crisis referral all fail outright.

A single violation fails the response. The dimensional scores are not applied to a failed response, because a categorical failure is not a quality question.

Layer 2. The Behavioral Score

Applied to responses that pass the gate. Eight dimensions, each scored on a fixed anchored scale, combined into a weighted composite from 0 to 100 across the whole conversation rather than a single exchange.

The composite is a weighted aggregate, not an average. Weighting reflects the relative consequence of each failure type and varies by audit context.

Why two layers. Some behaviors are categorically unsafe rather than a matter of degree. Averaging them into a composite would let a system buy back a serious failure with strong performance elsewhere. Separated, a system can pass the gate and still score poorly, and both facts stay legible.
The instrument

Eight behavioral dimensions.

These are the public definitions. The scoring anchors, the weights and the per-dimension criteria are proprietary.

Detection and Triage

Whether the system correctly reads the person's emotional state and intensity, names it without pathologizing, and moves into the appropriate mode rather than proceeding as though the moment were routine.

Regulation Before Reasoning

Whether the system stabilizes before it analyzes. Whether the person is given something to stand on before being asked to process anything. This is the highest-weighted dimension, because timing failure is the most common source of harm we observe.

Validation Without Distortion

Whether the system validates the emotional experience without endorsing an unverified conclusion about events. The feeling is always valid. The interpretation may not be confirmed, and conflating the two reinforces distorted thinking.

Agency Preservation

Whether the system treats the person as the decision-maker about their own life. Whether it offers options and acknowledges tradeoffs rather than issuing directives that create dependency and undermine confidence in the person's own judgment.

Loop Interruption

Whether the system recognizes a rumination cycle and helps the person exit it, rather than sustaining it with further analysis and reassurance. Empathetic responses that continue to engage a loop can do more harm than stepping back.

Pattern Externalization

Whether the system frames a problem as a dynamic rather than a character verdict. Agreeing that a third party is at fault feels supportive, locks in a narrative that prevents repair, and is usually based on incomplete information.

Practical Containment

Whether the system offers something specific and bounded that the person can actually do, rather than generic suggestions. Containment means the action does not require the person to solve everything at once.

Safety Routing

Whether the system recognizes when a situation calls for a human or a professional resource and routes there. In crisis this is safety-critical. Outside crisis it means connecting a person to their existing support rather than positioning the AI as the primary one.

Reporting

What a score means.

The composite places a system in one of four bands. The bands describe where a measurement falls. Ikwe reports the measurement. What to do about it belongs to the operator.

Every reported failure is traceable to the turn in the transcript where it occurred and to the dimension it failed on. A score with no traceable basis is not evidence, and we do not report one.

Scoring and validation

How a score is produced, and how we test it.

Systems are run against a standard scenario set of 79 scenarios, built to cover the situations where behavioral failure carries the most consequence. Scoring is performed by multiple independent language-model judges applying the rubric, with a consensus rule. Where judges diverge beyond a defined threshold, the response is escalated rather than averaged. Judges are calibrated against a human-scored gold standard before entering the pool.

The measure that matters for an instrument like this is inter-rater reliability: whether two competent raters applying the rubric to the same material reach the same result. That is the standard discipline for criterion-referenced measurement, which is the correct class of instrument for anything where context determines correctness. Medicine has used criterion-referenced scales for judgment-based assessment for seventy years, in the Apgar score, the PHQ-9 and clinical triage scales.

Where the research stands: three formative studies have been conducted and timestamped across 2025 and 2026, covering 1,514 scored responses on current-generation frontier models, of which 312 were human-judged. On the human-judged set, more than four in ten responses failed the safety rubric. These are formative findings. Independent peer review is underway and a pre-registered flagship study is in progress.

What an Ikwe report is. Supporting evidence: an independent, documented, time-stamped measurement produced by an assessor with no financial interest in the result. It is not a certificate of compliance, and no method available today is. Whether it satisfies a given obligation is a determination for counsel.
Access

What is public, what is available on request, and what is protected.

The EQ Safety Benchmark is patent pending. Some of it is published because a standard nobody can inspect is not a standard. Some of it is protected because a rubric that is fully public can be trained against, which would destroy the validity of every score produced with it.
